# Company Wars — Business-First Pivot

Status: **brainstorming document, not an approved design.** This file records a direction to
explore after the first playtest. It does not change any rule, decision, content, schema,
fixture, roadmap, or implementation. Existing systems named here are **retained candidates,
reopened, or parked** — not silently deleted.

This pivot is intentionally allowed to contradict the signed-off design. If the direction is
approved later, the affected locked decisions must be superseded explicitly in the append-only
decision log. That approval is not assumed here.

---

## 1. North star

**The player builds a business. During the automatic round, that business operates against
another business. The rooms, staff flow, customers, and consequences of the player's plan are
what they watch.**

The current version is closer to an employee auto-battler placed inside an office. Employees
are the active pieces; rooms mostly modify them; the building is the container. The desired
version reverses that hierarchy:

1. **The business is the build.** Floors and rooms define its engine.
2. **Employees make it run.** They are hired and visible, but the player may not need to place
   them individually.
3. **The founder determines the business fantasy.** Corporate, conbini, ryokan, onsen, and
   later businesses should not feel like cosmetic skins over the same roster.
4. **The automatic phase shows the plan working or failing.** A player should be able to see
   queues, empty workstations, exhausted workers, missing stock, or neglected guests without
   reading the event log first.

For the corporate/salaryman business, the fantasy is particularly clear:

> Lease another floor. Fit it with desks and supporting rooms. Hire more people. Push output
> harder than the organization can sustain. Decide whether to care that the workers are
> burning out before the quarter ends.

---

## 2. What is direction versus what is still a question

The playtest feedback gives a strong direction, but not yet a complete ruleset. Keeping that
distinction prevents this planning phase from locking in the first plausible implementation.

### Strong direction

- Founders are tied to distinct business types or business identities.
- Floors and rooms should matter more than individually positioned employees.
- Employees may move and assign themselves rather than being tactical pieces.
- Corporate is the first and only business to design deeply now.
- Corporate growth means floors, desks, headcount, output pressure, and burnout.
- Other businesses use different operational fantasies: a conbini cares about goods and
  customers; a ryokan about rooms and guests; an onsen about baths, condition, and visitors.
- The project is back in planning and brainstorming. Existing implementation is evidence and
  reusable infrastructure, not a constraint on the concept.

### Still open

- Whether employees are individual persistent characters, anonymous headcount, or a hybrid.
- Whether walking is mechanical, presentational, or both.
- Whether desks are individually placed, generated by a room, or represented by room upgrades.
- Whether competing businesses attack one another directly, compete for a shared market, or
  simply race on performance.
- Whether the current Sales / Poach / Scandal / Curse vocabulary still fits every business.
- Whether one founder maps to one unique business, or multiple founders can represent variants
  of the same business family.
- Whether the automatic phase is still called a fight or becomes a quarter, shift, operating
  period, trading day, or another business-specific name.

## 3. Recommended first model: rooms are decisions, staff are flow

The best starting hypothesis is a **room-first operations auto-battler**.

### What the player places

The player places rooms on floors. A room has:

- a footprint;
- a function;
- capacity;
- an operating cycle or throughput;
- staffing needs;
- inputs and outputs where relevant;
- upgrade or fit-out choices;
- relationships with nearby rooms;
- a persistent history such as Tenure, if Tenure survives the pivot.

Examples for corporate might include an Open Office, Sales Floor, Break Room, HR Office,
Server Room, Meeting Room, and Executive Office. The catalogue should begin much smaller
than this list; these are roles to explore, not approved content.

### What the player hires

The player hires enough people, and possibly the right categories of people, to operate the
rooms. Employees assign themselves according to visible rules. The player solves questions
such as:

- Do I have enough workers for all this floorspace?
- Which rooms receive scarce specialists first?
- Do I overstaff production or reserve recovery capacity?
- Can the elevators and support rooms handle another floor?
- Am I buying output now at the cost of exhaustion later?

This removes employee tile placement without reducing hiring to a meaningless number.
Staffing is still a build decision; it is just expressed through capacity, priorities, and
flow rather than manual coordinates.

### What the player watches

During the automatic phase, workers:

- arrive through the entrance or elevator;
- walk to workstations;
- operate rooms;
- take breaks or fail to take them;
- queue when a dependency is constrained;
- visibly tire, slow, slump, leave, or recover;
- move when room priorities change.

The important design rule is that animation reflects simulation state. A worker should not
walk across the building merely to make the screen lively while the simulation treats every
room as fully staffed. Conversely, the first prototype does **not** need full pathfinding.
Assignment and travel may be resolved abstractly, then visualized consistently.

## 4. Three employee models to compare

The phrase “employees are hired but walk around and do what they need to do” supports three
different designs. They should be compared before one is chosen.

| Model | How it works | Strength | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Pooled headcount** | Hire quantities by broad role. Rooms draw from a shared pool. Sprites visualize that pool | Simplest, makes the rooms unquestionably central | Workers may feel disposable and abstract; individual burnout is less legible |
| **Persistent individuals** | Every hire has identity, stamina, skill, and history, but assignment is automatic | Strong stories and readable burnout; hiring still matters | Can recreate the current employee-heavy complexity without manual placement |
| **Cohorts** *(recommended prototype)* | Hire small groups such as Salarymen, Sales Staff, or Managers; individual sprites are shown, but rules and upgrades mostly act on the cohort | Keeps visible people while reducing card and targeting complexity | Needs careful UI so a cohort does not feel like an arbitrary bundle |

The recommendation is a prototype choice, not a final answer. Start with cohorts because they
preserve “hire people and burn them out” while avoiding forty unique employee ability cards.
If even cohorts pull attention away from the rooms, fall back to pooled headcount.

## 5. Corporate vertical-slice fantasy

Corporate should be designed as a complete game before the generic business framework is
finalized. Building abstractions for four imagined businesses now would likely encode the
wrong common denominator.

### 5.1 Corporate's loop

1. **Lease capacity.** Open a new floor when the current one is constrained.
2. **Fit out production.** Build offices and add desk capacity.
3. **Build support.** Break rooms, HR, elevators, and other support consume space that could
   otherwise hold revenue-producing desks.
4. **Hire.** Add workers or specialists. Hiring increases potential output and recurring
   pressure.
5. **Set priorities.** Decide which rooms staff first and, potentially, how hard the firm
   pushes this quarter.
6. **Operate automatically.** Workers travel, work, tire, queue, and produce business output.
7. **Read the result.** Revenue, customers or contracts, burnout, wage burden, and bottlenecks
   explain why the firm won or lost.
8. **Live with the organization.** Floors, rooms, and possibly exhausted staff carry forward.

### 5.2 Burnout should be a system, not a minor debuff

A promising corporate engine is:

- Work creates output and fatigue.
- Greater intensity creates output faster and fatigue faster.
- Support rooms trade potential desk space for recovery or resilience.
- A tired workforce becomes inefficient before becoming unusable.
- Burnout has a business consequence: absence, turnover, errors, lost reputation, severance,
  or a combination selected during design.
- The fixed end of the round makes overwork potentially rational. Winning now can be worth
  damaging the next quarter.

This creates the desired dark salaryman satire without requiring the player to issue cruel
orders to individual named people. The system produces the cruelty from optimization
pressure.

### 5.3 Questions that must be settled for burnout

- Does fatigue reset between automatic rounds, partially recover, or persist completely?
- Can a worker burn out mid-round, or does burnout resolve afterward?
- Is replacing staff cheaper than supporting them, and if so, what stops replacement from
  becoming the single correct strategy?
- Are wages paid per round, represented by purchase price, or handled through upkeep?
- Does worker experience or room Tenure make replacement costly?
- Can the player set intensity? A continuous slider is risky because it may have one solved
  optimum. Discrete policies with visible trade-offs may be stronger: **Normal Hours**,
  **Mandatory Overtime**, **Death March**.
- What does the opponent do to this engine, if anything?

No stamina value, recovery rate, or penalty should be chosen until these questions have a
coherent answer.

## 6. Desks, furniture, and rooms

“Simplify to just rooms” and “fill them with desks and things” do not require individual
furniture placement. There are at least three interpretations:

| Approach | Player action | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Room includes furniture** | Place an Office | It arrives with a default desk layout based on capacity |
| **Fit-out upgrades** *(recommended first)* | Place an Office, then choose or buy a fit-out | Desks and equipment appear automatically; the room gains capacity or a specialization |
| **Selective key furniture** | Most furnishing is automatic; only rare strategic fixtures are placed | Preserves some spatial texture without retaining a full furniture shop |

Start with fit-out upgrades. It satisfies the visual fantasy of a floor filling with desks,
keeps rooms as the main decisions, and removes dozens of low-value drag operations. Key
furniture can return only if a prototype shows that rooms alone lack enough spatial texture.
Furniture and recipes should therefore be **parked**, not declared deleted.

## 7. What does it mean for businesses to “fight”?

This is the largest unanswered design question. “The business is doing the fighting” does
not necessarily mean rooms should inherit employee attack abilities. Three models fit the
vision:

### A. Parallel performance race

Both businesses operate for a fixed period. The one with the better result wins. The rival
creates pressure through the target score, but does not directly attack.

- **Pros:** clearest business fantasy; easy to understand across very different businesses.
- **Cons:** the opponent may feel like a scoreboard rather than an adversary.

### B. Shared market competition *(recommended starting direction)*

Both businesses operate, but draw from a shared opportunity: customers, contracts, demand,
or reputation. Better service captures more of it. The opponent affects the player because
both compete for the same market, not because a Legal employee shoots a debuff across the
street.

- **Pros:** businesses interact naturally; corporate, conbini, ryokan, and onsen can all
  compete for customers or value without identical combat animations.
- **Cons:** requires a fair comparison between businesses with very different operations.

### C. Direct interference

Businesses produce revenue and also sabotage, poach, scandalize, or curse the rival.

- **Pros:** preserves more of the current revenue-race combat and creates dramatic swings.
- **Cons:** risks keeping the old employee battler underneath a new building skin; some verbs
  will feel arbitrary for a conbini or ryokan.

The current Revenue and Loyalty presentation may remain useful under any model, but the
full Sales / Poach / Scandal / Curse rules should be **reopened**, not assumed unchanged.
Corporate plus conbini should be used as the first cross-business test: if the competition
is understandable between those two, the common match layer may be sound.

## 8. Business identity framework — provisional only

Do not force every business into one generic “stock” resource yet. Shared primitives should
be extracted only after corporate works and a conbini paper design challenges it.

A likely common structure is:

> **Acquire inputs → move them through rooms and staff → serve demand → earn value, while a
> business-specific constraint worsens.**

| Business | Core activity | Likely input | Primary constraint | Visible failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Corporate** | Produce billable work or contracts | Staff time | Fatigue / burnout | Empty desks, slumped workers, mistakes, turnover |
| **Conbini** | Stock shelves and serve customers | Goods and deliveries | Stock flow / queue time | Empty shelves, long queues, customers leaving |
| **Ryokan** | Turn over rooms and satisfy guests | Rooms, food, service time | Limited guest capacity / service quality | Dirty rooms, waiting guests, poor reviews |
| **Onsen** | Maintain baths and serve visitors | Heat, water, supplies | Condition / maintenance load | Closed bath, crowding, dissatisfied visitors |

These businesses should share UI and simulation primitives where it helps, but they must not
become identical production chains with renamed bars. A business type that requires one new
primitive is not automatically a design failure. The better technical goal is **a small
composable operations vocabulary**, not “all future businesses are data-only at any cost.”

### Founder mapping remains ambiguous

The feedback says each founder has their own business type. That could mean:

1. each founder represents one unique business;
2. each founder selects a business family, with multiple founders offering different takes
   on corporate or conbini;
3. business is selected separately, and founder modifies it.

Option 1 is the literal reading and should be the default assumption until clarified. It also
means the current eight founders may need new identities rather than being distributed two
per four business families. No mapping is proposed in this file.

## 9. Presentation of the automatic phase

The current facade battle was designed to show stationary employees firing from known tile
positions. If staff now walk through an operating building, that presentation must be
reconsidered.

A useful automatic-phase view must show:

- the rooms the player built;
- whether each room is staffed, blocked, recovering, or producing;
- worker movement and fatigue;
- the opponent's progress;
- the current win condition;
- enough causal detail to understand a loss.

Three presentation sketches are worth prototyping:

| View | Strength | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Two compact operating buildings side by side** | The competition is always visible | Ten floors of readable interiors will not fit comfortably on the current canvas |
| **Player building large, rival as a compact summary** | Makes the player's creation the star and preserves readable workers | The rival may not feel physically present |
| **Camera alternates or pans between businesses** | Each business can be shown at useful scale | Harder to compare and diagnose in real time |

A stacked set of full-size top-down floors should not be assumed to fit. Top-down interiors
stacked as a facade can also create a confused perspective. Before changing any screen spec,
make greybox mockups of all three views at the actual logical canvas and judge readability.
The exterior street can remain as an establishing shot if it supports the fantasy, but it
should not relegate the business the player built to a small hover inset.

The ledger and autopsy remain valuable candidates, but their categories will need to change
from per-employee attacks to room throughput, staffing, queues, fatigue, demand served, and
lost opportunities.

## 10. Spatial depth: do not assume walking distance solves it

Removing individual employee placement removes much of the current adjacency puzzle. Room
placement must earn enough depth to replace it.

Possible sources of room-first depth include:

- room adjacency and incompatibility;
- door and corridor access;
- elevator throughput;
- distance between dependent rooms;
- utilities or support coverage;
- noise, heat, cleanliness, or other local pressure;
- capacity versus resilience;
- persistent rooms that are expensive to move;
- irregular floor shapes and fixed structural features.

Simple distance from every room to the elevator is **not sufficient by itself**. It rewards
placing the most valuable room closest and can quickly become a solved ordering. It only
becomes interesting if rooms depend on different destinations, corridors constrain routes,
or movement capacity creates competing demands.

Likewise, real-time pathfinding should not be committed merely because workers are animated.
A staged approach is safer:

1. Prototype room placement with abstract staffing and no walking penalty.
2. Add one spatial dependency, such as elevator capacity or room-to-room supply distance.
3. Keep it only if floorplans now produce meaningfully different strategies.
4. Add visible movement that truthfully represents the retained rule.
5. Use runtime pathfinding only if simpler assignment and travel models cannot express the
   desired behavior.

## 11. Minimal corporate prototype

The first design target should be deliberately smaller than the current game. It is meant to
answer whether the pivot is fun, not preserve every shipped-intent system.

### Suggested contents

- 2–3 usable floors, with one initially open;
- 4 room functions at most: production, recovery, staffing/support, and one specialization;
- 2 staff cohorts at most: general workers and one specialist;
- room fit-out rather than placed furniture;
- one fatigue/burnout model;
- one staffing-priority model;
- one competitor archetype;
- one fixed-length operating round;
- clear throughput, fatigue, and bottleneck readouts.

### Explicitly parked for this prototype

- individual employee abilities;
- employee tile placement;
- detailed employee targeting;
- furniture placement;
- hidden recipes;
- the portal and extraplanar hires;
- campaign map complexity;
- multiple business implementations;
- final art and final screen layout.

“Parked” means the prototype does not need them. It does not mean this document has decided
to remove them from the final game.

### Questions the prototype must answer

1. Does leasing and fitting out another floor feel like growing a company?
2. Are room choices interesting without employee placement?
3. Does autonomous staffing produce understandable consequences rather than arbitrary ones?
4. Is burnout a tempting strategic debt, not merely a punishment meter?
5. Can the player diagnose an idle room or lost round by watching?
6. Does the opponent feel present under the chosen competition model?
7. After three rounds, does the player want to restructure the business for a reason other
   than simply buying the next numerical upgrade?

## 12. Impact on the existing design

This is an impact map, not an implementation plan.

| Existing area | Planning status under this pivot | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Multi-floor building and leasing | **Retain** | Central to the new fantasy |
| Rooms and persistent commitment | **Retain, redefine** | Rooms become the operating pieces rather than employee auras |
| Employees | **Reopen** | Hiring and visible workers stay; identity, abilities, placement, and persistence do not |
| Furniture | **Park** | Fit-out may replace it, but room-only depth must be tested first |
| Tenure | **Reopen** | Persistence still fits, but current “half the room's tiles occupied at commit” rule does not |
| Revenue race | **Reopen selectively** | Revenue and a fixed period fit; direct attack verbs may not |
| Client Loyalty | **Candidate to retain** | Could become customers, reputation, or contracts, but must work across businesses |
| Recipes | **Park** | Could return as room upgrades or discoveries after the core loop works |
| Portal / occult layer | **Park, do not erase** | Tone remains valuable, but it should not obscure the first corporate prototype |
| Ledger and autopsy | **Retain, rewrite categories** | Causal diagnosis remains essential |
| Battle presentation | **Redesign** | Stationary window occupants no longer represent the simulation |
| Build screen | **Redesign interaction, reuse structure where useful** | Shop, inspector, tower interaction, and staffing controls all change |
| Founder select | **Redesign meaning** | Founder now selects or strongly defines a business |
| Pure deterministic simulation | **Retain** | Nothing about an operations sim requires nondeterminism or floating point |
| Existing fixtures and balance model | **Do not touch during brainstorming** | They become migration work only after approval and a new spec |

The architectural pipeline may remain useful, but it would not survive “untouched.” Content
schema, snapshots, generators, harness populations, manifest entries, screen scenes, and
fixtures all encode the current unit-driven design and would require deliberate migration.

## 13. Planning sequence — no implementation yet

The next work should remain design work.

1. **Confirm the competition model.** Compare parallel race, shared market, and direct
   interference using one corporate-versus-corporate example.
2. **Confirm the employee abstraction.** Compare pooled headcount, persistent individuals,
   and cohorts using the same floorplan.
3. **Define one corporate operating loop.** Inputs, room actions, staffing, fatigue, output,
   and what carries between rounds — no detailed content catalogue yet.
4. **Draw three build-phase paper states.** Early, growing, and overextended. Check that each
   creates a real choice rather than an obvious upgrade order.
5. **Mock up the automatic phase at the real screen size.** Test the three presentation
   options from §9 before choosing cutaway, facade, or focus view.
6. **Write a conbini counter-design on paper.** Use it to discover which primitives are
   actually shared; do not implement it.
7. **Only then propose superseding decisions and a new formal spec.** Until that sign-off,
   no existing document, code, content, or fixture should be migrated.

---

## 14. Current recommendation

Prototype the game as **rooms plus autonomous staff cohorts**, with room fit-outs generating
desks and equipment visually. Make corporate output compete through a **shared market** for
a fixed quarter. Make burnout persistent enough that overtime can win this round and damage
the next. Keep employee movement visible and honest, but begin with simple assignment rather
than committing to runtime pathfinding. Show the player's building at readable scale and the
rival as a strong summary until an actual-size mockup proves two full interiors can coexist.

Most importantly, treat the current combat, furniture, recipes, and occult systems as parked
material rather than obligations. The purpose of the next prototype is to discover the game
this playtest pointed toward, not to make every previous system fit inside it.
